Pinewood Derby

January · Pinewood Derby recruiting event

Overview

The Pinewood Derby is a Scouting classic: each Cub Scout designs, builds, and paints a small wooden car (with an adult's help) and then races it down a track at a pack-wide event. Kids take an active role in every step, watch an idea of their own become a real object, and walk away with something they made. That is exactly the kind of hands-on, take-home fun that makes a great recruiting showcase.

Why it works for recruiting

A Derby is "show, don't sell" in action. Invite prospective families to come watch, and the event sells itself: the gym is loud, every kid is cheering, and a visiting child sees other kids racing cars they built. Siblings and guests are welcome to build and race their own cars (they just are not eligible for the registered-Scout trophies), so a prospective family can go from spectator to participant in the same afternoon. Pair the Derby with a follow-up invitation and it becomes a recruiting moment, not just a pack tradition.

The two-part structure

A successful Derby is really two events, and most of the work is in the first one:

  • Build workshops (two or three weekends before race day): families pick up a kit and design, cut, sand, paint, weight, and tune their cars at shared work stations. The workshops are where the real Scouting skill-building happens.
  • Race day: cars are checked in against the rules, raced in heats by den, and celebrated with awards. Plan for a brisk, well-staffed afternoon.

Who runs it

Form a small Pinewood Derby committee early and divide ownership. The roles that past packs found essential:

  • Event lead: owns the schedule, books the space, and books a race official to run the track and timing software.
  • Workshop lead: owns kits, materials, tools, and workshop space and volunteers.
  • Race-day lead: owns the run-of-show, volunteer sign-ups, check-in, and awards.
  • Track and electronics crew: a few detail-oriented people to assemble the track (level, same-length sections, screws tightened evenly) plus one or two to run the timer, cables, and computer.
Book the hard-to-get pieces first. The race date depends on the space (often a school gym) and a race official who owns the track and timing system. Lock those down roughly six months out, then schedule the build workshops backward from race day.

Calendar of Activities

This covers the long lead-up planning timeline and the run-of-show for race day itself. Adjust the day counts to your own calendar; the order matters more than the exact dates.

About 6 months out (180 days)

  • Set the race date in coordination with your race official and your venue so it works for both.
  • Book the race runner / official who owns the track and timing software. Aim for a setup that can run two to three races per minute.

About 5 months out (150 days)

  • Book the race space (a school gym works well; many councils also offer their space for Derby races).
  • Schedule the build workshops with your pack committee, working backward from race day. Allow two to three weekends before the race for Scouts to build.

About 2 months out (60 days)

  • Book the workshop space. A community workshop or makerspace is ideal because of the bandsaw and ventilation for painting.
  • Recruit your workshop and race-day volunteer crews (see the volunteer roles below).

About 1 month out (30 days)

  • Take inventory and purchase workshop materials (see the materials lists under Activities).
  • Buy car kits: one per Scout plus several extras for mistakes, siblings, and guests.
  • Confirm a scale is available at every build event so cars can be weighed as they are finished.

About 3 weeks out (21 days)

  • Hold the build workshops (design, cut, sand, paint, add weight, mount and tune wheels, weigh in).
  • Set up, run, and clean up each workshop with signs, tarps, drop cloths, and snacks.
  • Buy trophies, medals, and patches (allow lead time for any engraving).
  • Send pre-workshop communication to families, including staggered arrival times to avoid a rush on the bandsaw.

About 2 to 3 weeks out (14 to 20 days)

  • Design and print the voting ballots (recommended categories: Coolest, Most Creative, Funniest) or set up a QR-code voting form.
  • Open an Evite or SignUpGenius for race-day volunteer slots and any potluck snacks.
  • Decide your voting technology (paper ballots or an online form) and, if livestreaming, confirm the venue Wi-Fi name and password.
  • Prepare the “Our Next Adventures” follow-up cards: a printed card listing two or three dated, fun, guest-friendly upcoming events to hand to every prospective family.

About 1 week out (7 days)

  • Send the race roster (each Scout's name and den) to the race official, and plan to update it on race day for additions, siblings, and guests.
  • Finalize and circulate the race-day agenda.
  • Optional but recommended: hold car check-in at the pack meeting the week before so heats can be set in advance and race day runs tighter.

Race day: run of show

A clean, brisk schedule keeps young kids engaged. The pattern below stages older Scouts first, then younger Scouts, so racing starts quickly and no one waits long. (Times are an example for a midday start.)

12:00 pmArrival and check-in for older dens (Arrow of Light, Webelos, Bears).

Hold voting open only after every car is checked in, and tally votes during the racing so winners are ready by the awards. Aim for about two hours of racing even for a large pack (more than 50 kids); a staggered start for the youngest racers keeps the energy up.

After the event

  • Notify the venue custodian when cleanup is done (a small courtesy that keeps your space relationship healthy).
  • Post photos and a follow-up "join us" message for any prospective families who attended (see Advertising & Marketing).
  • Capture lessons learned while they are fresh (see the tips below).

Advertising & Marketing

Derby promotion works on two fronts: inside the pack (so families build cars and show up to race) and outward to the community (so prospective families come watch and get invited to join).

One "start your engines" email to families

Send families a single, do-it-all message that serves as the one-stop shop for the Derby. Include:

  • The build workshops: what happens (pick up kit, design, cut, paint, then later wheels, weights, and weigh-in), plus dates, locations, and what to wear (Class B shirts with a smock or grunge clothes for painting).
  • The race: date, time, location, and the award categories (fastest, coolest, funniest, most creative).
  • The race-day schedule so families know their den's check-in and race times.
  • A volunteer call: name the crews you need (pit crew, start and finish line, setup and takedown, concessions) and note that no experience is required.
  • The fine print: the car rules and specifications (see Activities), so there are no surprises at check-in.

Reaching prospective families (recruiting)

  • Make the race an open house. Invite prospective families to come watch, and tell them siblings and guests can build and race a car too. A loud, joyful gym full of kids is the best advertisement Scouting has.
  • Use your normal recruiting channels: the school "digital backpack," PTA newsletters, community Facebook groups, and word of mouth from current families. Ask each Scout to invite a friend.
  • Capture and follow up. Collect contact info from interested families at the event and follow up within a couple of days with a warm note and a specific invitation to your next meeting or event.
  • Photograph everything. Candids of kids mid-race (with photo permission) fuel social posts and next year's flyer. A short "join Cub Scouts, here is how" post with a link and a few photos converts after the fact.

Voting and engagement on the day

Audience-choice voting keeps spectators (including visiting families) involved. Print ballots, or post a QR code around the gym that opens an online voting form. Give every Scout and their guests a vote, and use ranked choice (ask for a second choice) so ties are easy to break.

Activities

The Derby has two activity phases: the build workshops (where the Scouting skills happen) and race day (where the cars compete). Below are the stations to run, the car rules to share, the materials to gather, and hard-won tips from past races.

Build workshop stations

Set up the workshop as a flow of stations so families move from raw kit to race-ready car. Staff each station with at least one or two volunteers, and stagger arrival times so the cutting station does not back up.

Station What kids do Typical materials
Design Sketch a car shape on paper before cutting. Paper, pencils, rulers, design idea books.
Cutting (adult-run) Hand off a marked block; an adult cuts the shape on the bandsaw. Bandsaw, safety glasses, an experienced adult operator. This is the one station kids do not run.
Shaping & sanding Smooth the cut car with rasps and sandpaper. Rasps, sanding blocks, sandpaper in several grits.
Painting Prime, paint, and decorate the car. Acrylic and spray paint, brushes, water cups, drop cloths, tarps, ventilation.
Wheels & axles Mount wheels, drill straight pilot holes with the jig, and free up the axles. Axle jig, drill and bits, hammer, super glue, graphite, extra wheels and axles.
Weight & weigh-in Add weight to approach (but not exceed) 5 ounces, then weigh the car. Kitchen scale, stick-on or press-in weights, a dimension checker.

An optional axle-polishing station is worth adding: smoother axles are a meaningful, hands-on way for kids to make their cars faster. Bring a longer section of track to the workshop so cars can be test-run and any that do not reach the bottom can be fixed early. A car that stalls on race day is a real disappointment.

Race-day stations and volunteer crews

Race day for a 50-plus car pack needs a deep volunteer bench. Break the work into small, clearly defined crews so no one is overloaded:

  • Setup crew: assemble the track, check-in station, fix-it station, tables, and decorations.
  • Check-in crew: confirm each car meets the weight and dimension rules, assign a number, mark it on the roster, and place it on deck.
  • Pit / repair crew: make on-the-spot repairs and adjustments so every car can finish its races.
  • Starting line crew: get the right cars to the track for each heat, on time.
  • Finish line crew: shuttle cars from the finish back to their pre-race parking spot. A path beside the track for these runners keeps things fast and avoids collisions. Older Scouts make great runners.
  • Concessions crew: set up, run, and break down snacks for Scouts and families.
  • Voting crew: distribute ballots (or post the QR code), then gather and count votes.
  • Breakdown crew: tear down the track, fold and store tables, and clean up.

Awards

Recognize speed and creativity so every kind of car has a chance to win something:

  • Participation: a patch for every Scout, plus a first-Derby medal for newcomers.
  • Speed by den: 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place ribbons or medals within each den.
  • Speed overall: 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place trophies for the whole pack.
  • Audience choice: Coolest Car, Most Creative Car, and Funniest Car trophies, decided by the vote.

Consider widening the award categories so more cars get called out, and remember that siblings and guests can earn bragging rights among non-Scout racers even though they are not eligible for the registered-Scout trophies.

Car rules and specifications (the "fine print")

Share these with families well before race day so cars pass check-in. Cars are inspected at check-in; a car that fails can be adjusted and resubmitted until registration closes. Once checked in, cars stay at the judge's table until racing and tabulation are done (a broken car may be fixed and rejoin).

General rules

  • Scouts build a new car each year.
  • Wheels and axles must be, or closely resemble, the official kit wheels and axles. A generic replacement for a lost wheel is fine, but no different designs.
  • Wheel bearings, washers, and bushings are prohibited.
  • Only dry lubricant is permitted (graphite, not oil or WD-40).
  • The car must be freewheeling, with no starting device, and must not ride on springs.
  • Details like steering wheels, drivers, lights, and decorations are allowed if they are secured and do not push the car past the size or weight limits.

Dimensions and weight

  • Width: 2 3/4 inches.
  • Length: 7 inches.
  • Total height: no more than 4 1/2 inches.
  • Width between wheels: 1 3/4 inches.
  • Minimum clearance underneath: 3/8 inch (critical, so the car clears the track's center rail).
  • Weight: no more than 5.0 ounces total, including everything. Weight is the biggest factor in speed, so build toward 5 ounces without going over. A scale will be available at the race for last-minute adjustments.

Wheels and axles (the trickiest part)

Sand any burrs off the axles and the wheel surfaces that touch the track, and adjust the axles so the wheels spin freely. Axles can pop loose, especially when set in the kit's bottom slots rather than drilled pilot holes, so a dab of glue helps (keep it off the wheels). Graphite powder on the axles speeds the wheels up. Plan to have an axle puller and graphite on hand at the race.

Tips from past races

Lessons packs have learned the hard way:

  • Keep food simple. Snacks instead of a full meal cut cleanup and worked fine.
  • Check cars in the night before at a pack meeting where possible, so heats are set in advance and race day flows without confusion or missed cars.
  • Test on real track at the workshop. Bring a longer track section so slow or stalling cars get caught and fixed early.
  • Add an axle-polishing station, and print a sheet of speed tips for families.
  • Use older Scouts as runners, with a clear path beside the track. It is faster and safer.
  • Photograph each Scout with their car at check-in, and do quick trackside interviews. Kids love it and it makes great content.
  • A portable speaker and a charged mic matter more than you expect for a long program; keep spare batteries on hand.
  • Optional fundraiser: a small buy-in open category for parents, guests, and leaders (or a 50/50 raffle) adds fun and raises a little money.
