Community Campfire

August / September · Early school-year recruiting event

Overview

A Cub Scout campfire is a gathering of Scouts, leaders, and families for songs, short skits, stories, and a treat. It does not strictly have to happen after dark, or even with a real fire if conditions do not allow. The spirit of the campfire is camaraderie, not the flame itself, so battery lanterns or work lights under an awning are fine substitutes during a burn ban or bad weather.

Keep it short

Keep it short and brisk for this age group. The sweet spot is 30 to 45 minutes, since a full day of camping already tires young kids out. It is tempting to turn a campfire into an extended songfest, but resist that and always leave the audience wanting more. One rule is specific to Cubs: a Cub Scout campfire should be hosted by an adult (unlike older Scout campfires, which the youth can run themselves).

Use the fire as your pacing guide

The program works best when you use the fire as a pacing guide. High flames call for energetic songs and skits. As the fire burns lower, transition to slower songs and reflective moments, closing with a calm song or a short Cubmaster's Minute. Assigning a few simple roles ahead of time keeps things moving:

  • Master-of-the-Campfire: the emcee and planner.
  • Song-Master: leads the songs.
  • Cheer-Master: runs cheers and applauses.
  • Keeper-of-the-Flame: focused solely on the fire.

Content standards

This is a family audience, so set the tone in advance: avoid skits or jokes that target Scouts, leaders, or parents, allow no bathroom humor or dirty jokes, and screen and approve all skits, songs, and stories beforehand.

Calendar of Activities

This covers both the lead-up planning timeline and the run-of-show for the night itself.

3 to 4 weeks before

  • Pick a date, a location, and a rain or no-fire backup plan.
  • Assign the emcee, song-master, cheer-master, and fire keeper. The fire keeper should be trained and ideally hold the Firem'n Chit.
  • Start collecting song, skit, and story sign-ups from dens. Many packs use a campfire program planner sheet where each den or individual writes down their song, skit, or story. The official BSA Campfire Program Planner (form 33696) is a free template for this.

1 to 2 weeks before

  • Build the program order (see the run-of-show below) and time each act.
  • Confirm the s'mores supply list and who is bringing what.
  • Send the main advertising push to families.

Day before and day of (setup)

  • Gather fire safety gear: water, sand, and shovels, kept nearby at all times.
  • Prep the fire site using an existing ring where possible to follow Leave No Trace.
  • Lay out roasting sticks, food, hand wipes, and printed song lyric sheets.

The campfire (run-of-show, about 30 to 45 minutes)

  • Opening (3 to 5 min): the formal opening often coincides with lighting the fire or a dramatic entrance by leaders. Introduce the song-master and cheer-master.
  • Build-up (15 to 20 min): fast, loud, action songs, short skits, and cheers between acts.
  • Wind-down (5 to 10 min): a slower song, a short story, and a Cubmaster's Minute.
  • S'mores and closing (10 min): roast marshmallows, then a quiet goodnight song to dismiss.

For ordering acts: put noisy acts early and calmer elements later, keep your best act for last and your second-best first, and avoid long pauses between acts.

Advertising & Marketing

The goal is to get families excited, gather sign-ups for performances, and make sure people show up hungry for s'mores.

  • Announce at the pack meeting and in your pack's usual channel (email, Scoutbook, or app) with the date, time, location, and what to bring (camp chairs, a jacket, a flashlight).
  • Send a simple flyer home with Scouts and post it digitally.
  • Circulate the sign-up and program planner sheet early so dens can claim a song, skit, or story. This doubles as advertising because it gets families invested.
  • Promote the food specifically. Let everyone know beforehand that you have food planned so they leave stomach room and get even more excited.
  • Ask new families for a favorite song. When new Scouts join, ask whether they have a favorite camp song to share, a nice way to fold newcomers in.

Activities

Songs (the backbone)

Choose songs by tempo to match the fire: kick off with lively, rowdy songs and action songs that use hand motions, feature medium-tempo songs in the main portion, and transition to slower, reflective songs as the fire winds down. Cub-friendly picks:

  • Fast / action: "The Little Green Frog," "Alive, Awake, Alert, Enthusiastic" (repeat it and get faster each time), and "Hey Ho Captain Jack."
  • Closing / quiet: "Scout Vespers" is a classic wind-down, followed by a "Goodnight" dismissal song where you send the dens off one group at a time.

Tip: print lyrics for anyone unfamiliar with the songs, and have leaders sing first. Kids warm up fast once an adult breaks the ice.

Skits, stories, and cheers (the filler between songs)

Keep them tight: skits should run about 90 seconds to 3 to 5 minutes, and stories should be 3 to 4 minutes at most to hold interest. Short run-ons and cheers from the cheer-master fill the gaps and keep the energy up.

S'mores

Come prepared so the treat goes smoothly. You will need a roasting stick for every attendee, plus plenty of marshmallows, graham crackers, and chocolate. Pack extra sticks, have a trash bag and hand wipes ready, and let adults handle anything near the flames. If you want to go bigger, Dutch oven desserts are an optional add-on.

Fire safety basics

Keep water, sand, and shovels on hand, and only the Keeper-of-the-Flame should tend the fire. Teach the Cubs this rule explicitly, along with never throwing anything into the flames.
