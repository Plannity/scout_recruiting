Cub Scout Recruiting

Clearly structured playbooks to help Cub Scout leaders run recruiting efforts in a simple, direct way.

Scouting is a time-tested program that offers kids and families many benefits. For kids, Scouting is fun, builds friendships, and offers exposure to adventures they don't get from other clubs. For families, Scouting gets kids off screens and outdoors, and builds strong minds and bodies.

The materials on this site are designed to offer Cub Scout leaders clearly structured playbooks to manage recruiting efforts in a simple, direct way. It relies on the first-hand knowledge of experienced leaders who ran successful packs and troops and is easy to adapt for your pack.

Recruiting elementary school children for Cub Scouts is fundamentally different from recruiting middle school children for Scouting. Elementary school children require communication and engagement with parents while middle school ages respond best from direct outreach from friends and peers. These recruiting strategies are tailored for elementary-age children.

Fun-First Recruiting

1. The Big Idea: Show, Don't Sell

Most recruiting nights are built backward. They put the ask first with a presentation, a sign-up table, an application, a payment, and hope that parents decide for their kids. Families came to find out whether their child might like Scouting, and instead they got pitched.

Flip the order. Let kids have real Scout fun first. A child who launches a rocket, races a car down a track, and walks out holding something they made does not need to be sold on Scouting, they have already decided. They will do the selling for you, on the car ride home.

The goal of the event is not “applications collected tonight.” The goal is kids saying “when can I come back?” plus a simple way for parents to register.

This is not wishful thinking; it is how families actually join. Scouting's own surveys point the same way: families overwhelmingly join because someone personally invited them (a friend, a current Scout, a neighbor) not because they sat through a pitch. And a large share of families who never join simply say no one ever asked, or never gave them a reason to care. Fun is the reason to care. A great experience turns a kid into a recruiter and gives a parent something easy to say yes to.

Know your audience

For Cub Scouts there are two customers in the room, and they need different things:

  • The child must have fun: Five-to-ten-year-olds decide with their hands and their gut. Fun is the entire argument.
  • The parent must see the vision: They sign, they pay, and in Cub Scouting they show up every week. Parents are not won by hype, they are won by a warm welcome, clear logistics, and watching their kid light up. The message for parents:
    • “Scouting gets kids off screens and outdoors.”
    • “Scouting teaches values like being trustworthy, friendly, courteous, and kind.”
    • “Scouting builds strong minds and bodies.”

So the event has one job for each person: win the child's heart, and hand the parent an easy, low-pressure yes. Everything that follows is built around those two jobs.

2. The Three Principles

Principle 1: Kids generate the pull

Activities should be obvious and self-directed. A child should be able to immediately start doing the activity with the minimum of instructions or rules. No opening assembly, no “let me tell you about Scouting.” Hands on equipment, immediate fun, and something to take home.

Principle 2: Parents get a 60-second yes, not a pitch

A parent watching their kid have a blast is already three-quarters convinced. Do not talk them back out of it with a sales pitch. Give them three answers and a way in:

  1. What does it cost? Have the real number, and the financial-aid answer, ready.
  2. How much time? Be specific and honest, for example, one den meeting and one pack activity a month.
  3. What's the next fun thing, and when? This is the hook, see Principle 3.

Then hand them a QR code to your BeAScout page and let the kid's face do the rest.

Principle 3: Close on the next adventure, not “sign here”

The strongest close is not “join tonight.” The strongest close is “we're launching rockets at the park on the 14th, want to join our pack?” You are selling the next dose of fun; membership is simply how a family keeps getting it. A kid already looking forward to the next event is far easier to enroll than one being asked to commit to an abstraction.

4. Choosing Fun That Actually Works (Ages 5–10)

Cubs are short on patience and long on enthusiasm. Pick activities with these traits and you can hardly go wrong:

  • Low skill floor, instant success: every kid succeeds on the first try, no waiting to “get good.”
  • Something to DO, not watch: a line of kids watching one kid is a dead station. Aim for parallel activity.
  • A take-home: a rocket, a Pinewood Derby car, or a craft. The object becomes a story and a reminder.
  • A pinch of awe: flight, fire, speed, a satisfying launch. Wonder is sticky.

You do not need ten stations. Four great ones beat ten mediocre ones.

5. The “Earn the Ask” Engine

The one way a fun day fails is the enthusiasm leak: forty thrilled kids go home, nobody captured anything, and by Monday it is gone. Plug the leak with four simple parts.

Capture, don't close

Set one low-pressure goal for the event: get every interested family's name and contact, usually with a quick lead card or by scanning your BeAScout QR. No one has to “decide” tonight. You are earning the right to follow up.

Send them home with something

Every kid leaves holding a thing they made or won. It is a physical reminder and a dinner-table story, “look what I made at Scouts.” That object keeps working for days after the event.

Put the next adventure in their hands

Give every family a printed “Our Next Adventures” card with two or three dated, fun, guest-friendly events. The invitation is concrete and low-stakes: come to the next one. Families who return once almost always join.

Follow up within 72 hours (warm, not salesy)

Decide tonight who contacts each family, and by when. The message is fun-forward: “Your son built an awesome catapult on Saturday! Our next adventure is a campfire night on the 14th, we'd love to have you. Here's the link if you'd like to join the pack.” Joining should feel like the easy next step, not a leap.

Make joining itself frictionless. Have your BeAScout link and QR ready, plus a volunteer who can walk a family through the online application in two minutes, offered, never pushed.

6. Volunteer Roles for a Fun-First Event

Notice what is not here: a “closer” parked at a sign-up table as the centerpiece. Reassign that energy to fun and follow-up.

  • Station Leaders: run the fun. One or two per station. Their only job is energy and flow. Give the shortest explanation possible (zero is best) and let the kids start having fun.
  • Fun Ambassadors (Floaters): circulate among parents, answer the three questions, read the room. Warm and casual, never cornering.
  • Capture Lead: owns the lead cards and QR sign-ins. Makes sure no interested family leaves uncaptured.
  • Next-Adventure & Follow-up Lead: owns the “next adventures” card and the 72-hour follow-up list. Arguably the most important role, this is where sign-ups are actually won.
  • Photographer: candids of kids mid-fun (with photo permission). Fuel for social posts and for next year's flyer.

7. Failure Modes to Avoid

  • The enthusiasm leak: a great event with no capture and no follow-up. The most common and most costly mistake.
  • The parent ambush: cornering parents at a table or with a hard sell. It sours the vibe and undoes the fun.
  • The watching station: one kid doing, ten kids waiting. Redesign for parallel participation or add capacity.
  • Over-programming: a rigid schedule that herds kids. Let them roam between stations; freedom is part of the fun.
  • Pitching the program, not the experience: parents care what their kid will feel, not your org chart.

8. Did It Work? (Better Metrics)

Judge the event over the following month, not at 8 p.m. that night.

  • Leads captured (interested families with contact info)
  • Percent followed up within 72 hours (aim for 100%)
  • Kids who asked to come back, or showed up to the next adventure
  • New youth applications in the four weeks after the event
  • Returning-family rate at your next one or two events
Bottom line: If kids left wanting more, you ran a great event. The applications will follow.
